The Basics

What is Easter?

Easter is one of the world's most widely celebrated holidays, observed by over 2 billion Christians and embraced culturally by many more around the globe. It falls each spring — on the first Sunday after the first full moon following the March equinox — meaning the date shifts each year, usually landing between late March and late April.

At its heart, Easter is a Christian religious holiday commemorating the resurrection of Jesus Christ, as described in the New Testament. Christians believe that Jesus, who was crucified on Good Friday, rose from the dead three days later — a foundational event that symbolises hope, life over death, and spiritual renewal.

Over the centuries, Easter has also absorbed folk traditions, seasonal customs, and cultural festivities — from painted eggs to chocolate bunnies — making it a holiday that blends the sacred and the joyful in a uniquely rich way.

The Past

A History of Easter

Easter has deep and layered roots — stretching from ancient pre-Christian spring festivals all the way to the elaborate traditions we know today.

Ancient Times — Pre-Christian Spring Festivals

Long before Easter, ancient peoples celebrated the arrival of spring with festivals honouring fertility, renewal, and the return of the sun. The Germanic goddess Ēostre was celebrated at the spring equinox with symbols of new life — eggs, rabbits, and blossoming flowers — which would later merge into Easter traditions.

c. 30 AD — The Crucifixion & Resurrection

Christians believe Jesus of Nazareth was crucified in Jerusalem during the Jewish Passover period, and rose from the dead three days later. This event became the cornerstone of the Christian faith and the foundation of the Easter celebration.

2nd Century — Early Christian Celebrations

Early Christians began commemorating the resurrection, often tying it to Passover. Debates arose about the precise date, leading to the "Easter Controversy" — different communities celebrated on different days until a common date was sought.

325 AD — The Council of Nicaea

The Council of Nicaea standardised the date of Easter — establishing that it would fall on the first Sunday after the first full moon after the spring equinox. This formula is still used today.

Middle Ages — Rich Church Traditions

Easter became the most important day in the Christian liturgical calendar, with elaborate church ceremonies, fasting during Lent, and midnight vigils. Eggs were saved during Lent then gifted and eaten joyfully at Easter.

16th–19th Century — Folk Customs & the Easter Bunny

German immigrants brought the legend of the "Osterhase" (Easter Hare) to America. Children made nests for this magical hare to fill with coloured eggs — the precursor to today's Easter basket. Egg decorating became an elaborate folk art, especially in Eastern Europe.

1873 — The First Chocolate Easter Egg

British chocolate maker Fry's created the first mass-produced chocolate Easter egg. Cadbury and others followed, transforming Easter into the confectionery tradition we know today.

Today — A Global Celebration

Easter is celebrated across six continents in hundreds of different ways — from solemn church services to egg hunts, lamb feasts to carnival parades. It remains both a profound religious observance and a joyful cultural celebration.

Around the World

How People Celebrate Easter

Easter traditions vary enormously across cultures and countries, blending religious observance with folk customs and pure springtime joy.

Church Services

From midnight Easter Vigils to sunrise services, Christians attend special ceremonies marking the resurrection. Cathedrals fill with flowers, candles, and triumphant hymns.

Egg Hunts

Children search gardens, parks, and homes for hidden Easter eggs — chocolate, plastic, or hand-decorated. One of the most beloved springtime traditions worldwide.

Decorating Eggs

A tradition especially vibrant in Eastern Europe — Ukrainian pysanky eggs feature intricate wax-resist designs. In Scandinavia, eggs hang on branches as Easter trees.

The Easter Bunny

Children await the Easter Bunny, who brings baskets of chocolate, sweets, and small gifts. Originating from German folklore, this tradition has spread across the world.

Processions & Parades

In Spain, Italy, and Latin America, elaborate Holy Week processions fill the streets. In Bermuda, colourful kites fill the sky on Good Friday.

Spring Festivals

In Scandinavia, Easter is celebrated with decorated birch branches and the tradition of children dressing as Easter witches, exchanging cards for sweets.

Easter Bonfires

In Germany, the Netherlands, and Scandinavia, large Easter bonfires are lit on Holy Saturday to ward off winter and welcome the light of spring.

Family Gatherings

Across the world, Easter is an occasion for family meals, gifts, and togetherness. Spring lamb, roasted meats, and festive baked goods are shared at the table.

Symbolism

Easter's Iconic Symbols

Each Easter symbol carries its own beautiful meaning — blending ancient traditions with Christian significance.

The Egg

A universal symbol of new life, the egg represents the sealed tomb and the promise of resurrection.

The Bunny

Rabbits — symbols of fertility and spring — became associated with Easter through Germanic folk traditions.

Spring Flowers

Lilies, tulips, and daffodils symbolise new life — the lily representing purity and resurrection in Christian tradition.

The Dove

A symbol of peace and the Holy Spirit, doves represent hope and new beginnings.

The Cross

The central symbol of Christianity — representing crucifixion and, through Easter, the triumph of life over death.

The Candle

The Easter Paschal candle symbolises Christ as the light of the world breaking through darkness.

The Lamb

The "Lamb of God" — one of the oldest Easter symbols, connecting Jesus to the sacrificial lamb of Passover.

The Rising Sun

Sunrise services celebrate dawn as the symbol of resurrection — light returning after the darkness.

On the Table

Traditional Easter Foods

No Easter is complete without the wonderful foods that vary by region — from rich chocolate treats to ancient breads and celebratory feasts.

Chocolate Eggs & Bunnies

The undisputed champion of modern Easter — a tradition since the 19th century. From hollow foil-wrapped eggs to elaborate filled creations, chocolate is the heart of contemporary Easter celebration.

Hot Cross Buns

Spiced, sweet buns marked with a cross — traditionally eaten on Good Friday in England. Their origins trace back to the 12th century, making them one of Easter's oldest food traditions.

Roast Lamb

The classic Easter Sunday centrepiece across much of Europe and the Middle East. Lamb carries deep symbolic meaning as the Paschal lamb — fitting and delicious in equal measure.

Easter Breads

From Italian colomba to Greek tsoureki and Russian kulich, enriched Easter breads across Europe are deeply tied to the joy of breaking the Lenten fast.

Pashka & Cheesecakes

In Eastern Orthodox countries, pashka — a moulded sweet cheese dessert — is a highlight of the Easter table, often stamped with the letters "XB" meaning Christ is Risen.

Spring Vegetables & Salads

Fresh spring vegetables — asparagus, peas, spinach, and new potatoes — feature prominently at Easter tables across northern Europe, celebrating the season's first harvest.
